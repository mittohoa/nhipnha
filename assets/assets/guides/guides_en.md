=== choking
# Choking
> If they can still cough, let them cough. If they can't cough, speak or breathe: back blows and abdominal thrusts now.

!! Call 115 if the person becomes unresponsive, turns blue, or the object won't come out.

## Adults and children over 1
1. Ask "Are you choking?". If they can cough or speak, encourage strong coughing — **don't** slap their back.
2. If they can't cough, speak or breathe: stand behind, lean them forward, give **5 firm back blows** between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand.
3. If still blocked: **5 abdominal thrusts** — arms around from behind, fist just above the navel, pull sharply in and up.
4. Alternate 5 back blows and 5 thrusts until the object comes out or they become unresponsive.
5. Pregnant or very large people: **chest** thrusts instead of abdominal thrusts.

## Babies under 1
1. Lay the baby face down along your forearm, head lower than the body, supporting the jaw.
2. **5 back blows** between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand.
3. Turn the baby over and give **5 chest thrusts** with two fingers in the middle of the chest, just below the nipple line.
4. Repeat until the object comes out. **No** abdominal thrusts for babies. **Don't** sweep the mouth blindly.

## If unresponsive
- Call 115, lay them on a firm surface and start **CPR**. Each time you open the mouth for breaths, look for the object.
- See a doctor afterwards even if the object came out (thrusts can cause internal injury).

## Sources
- IFRC — International First Aid, Resuscitation and Education Guidelines 2020

=== burns
# Burns
> Cool the burn under cool running water for 20 minutes — as soon as possible.

!! Call 115 for large burns, burns to the face/airway, electrical burns, deep chemical burns, or if the person is drowsy.

## Do now
1. Remove from the heat; electrical burns: **switch off the power first**.
2. **Cool (not ice-cold) running water for 20 minutes.** Keep the rest of the body warm (especially children and older people).
3. Remove rings, watches and clothing near the burn before it swells — unless stuck to the skin.
4. Cover loosely with cling film or a clean non-fluffy cloth.
5. Chemical burns: rinse continuously for at least 20 minutes; remove contaminated clothing.

## Don't
- Don't apply toothpaste, fish sauce, oil, butter, egg white or ice.
- Don't pop blisters.

## See a doctor if
- The burn is bigger than the person's palm, or deep (white, charred, painless).
- On the face, hands, feet, genitals or joints.
- The person is a young child, older adult, or has a chronic illness.

## Sources
- IFRC — International First Aid Guidelines 2020
- WHO — Burns fact sheet

=== bleeding
# Bleeding and nosebleeds
> Press firmly and directly on the wound with a clean cloth for at least 10 minutes.

!! Call 115 if blood spurts, bleeding doesn't stop after 10 minutes of pressure, or the person is faint, cold and sweaty.

## Bleeding wound
1. Use gloves or a plastic bag if you have them. **Press firmly and directly** on the wound with gauze or a clean cloth.
2. Keep pressing **for at least 10 minutes** without checking. If soaked, add more cloth on top — don't remove the first layer.
3. Raise the injured part above the heart if you can.
4. Object stuck in the wound: **don't pull it out**; press around it and keep it still.
5. A tourniquet is only for life-threatening limb bleeding that pressure can't stop — note the time.

## Nosebleed
1. Sit up and **lean forward** (not back — blood runs into the throat).
2. Pinch the soft part of the nose for **10–15 minutes**, breathing through the mouth.
3. See a doctor if it lasts over 20 minutes, follows a head injury, or the person takes blood thinners.

## Sources
- IFRC — International First Aid Guidelines 2020

=== seizure
# Seizures
> Keep them safe, don't hold them down, don't put anything in their mouth. Time the seizure.

!! Call 115 if it lasts over 5 minutes, seizures repeat, they don't wake up, are injured, in water, pregnant, or it's their first seizure.

## During the seizure
1. Stay calm and **note the time**.
2. Move hard or sharp objects away; cushion the head.
3. **Don't** restrain them, **don't** put a spoon, chopsticks or fingers in the mouth, **don't** give drinks.
4. Loosen tight collars.

## Afterwards
- Place them in the recovery position so saliva can drain.
- Stay until fully awake; speak calmly and reassure.
- Children with fever seizures: still see a doctor afterwards.

## Sources
- WHO — Epilepsy fact sheet
- IFRC — International First Aid Guidelines 2020

=== fainting
# Fainting
> Lay them flat and raise both legs. Most people wake within a minute.

!! Call 115 and start CPR if they don't wake and aren't breathing normally.

## Do now
1. Help them lie down somewhere safe and **raise both legs** about 30 cm.
2. Loosen tight clothing, get fresh air.
3. Once awake: sit up slowly; give water only when fully alert.
4. Not waking but **breathing**: recovery position, call 115.

## See a doctor if
- They fainted during exertion, or with chest pain, palpitations or breathlessness.
- They're older, have heart disease, or are pregnant.
- It happens repeatedly, or they hit their head.

## Sources
- IFRC — International First Aid Guidelines 2020

=== drowning
# Drowning
> Reach or throw, don't go: pass a pole or float from the edge. Don't jump in unless trained.

!! Call 115. If they aren't breathing: give 5 rescue breaths, then CPR.

## Do now
1. **Shout for help and call 115.** Get the person out of the water safely.
2. Check breathing. **Not breathing**: 5 rescue breaths first, then 30 compressions : 2 breaths until help arrives.
3. **Don't** hold them upside down or try to drain water — it wastes time and risks choking.
4. Breathing: recovery position, keep warm, watch until help arrives.
5. Everyone who nearly drowned should see a doctor (breathing trouble can appear hours later).

## Prevention
- Never leave children alone near water — including buckets, basins and tanks at home.
- Teach children to swim and water safety.

## Sources
- WHO — Drowning fact sheet
- IFRC — International First Aid Guidelines 2020

=== stroke
# Stroke — spot it with BE FAST
> Suspect a stroke: call 115 immediately and note when it started. Every minute counts.

!! Call 115 at any one of these signs — even if they go away.

## BE FAST
- **B (Balance)** — sudden loss of balance, dizziness, staggering.
- **E (Eyes)** — sudden blurred or lost vision in one or both eyes.
- **F (Face)** — face drooping on one side when smiling.
- **A (Arm)** — weakness or numbness on one side; one arm drifts down when both are raised.
- **S (Speech)** — slurred speech, trouble speaking or understanding.
- **T (Time)** — call 115 now and **note the time** signs began.

## While waiting
- Let them lie with the head slightly raised; if vomiting, lie on the side.
- **Don't** give food, drink or medicines (including blood-pressure pills or traditional remedies).
- **Don't** prick fingertips or do "cạo gió" — it wastes precious time.
- Take a list of their medicines (see the Emergency card in the app).

## Sources
- American Stroke Association — BE FAST
- WHO — Stroke

=== heart_attack
# Heart attack — warning signs
> Chest pain or pressure lasting more than a few minutes, spreading to the arm/jaw/back, cold sweat: call 115.

!! Call 115 now. Don't drive yourself to hospital.

## Common signs
- Pain, tightness or pressure in the chest lasting over a few minutes, or coming and going.
- Pain spreading to the arm, shoulder, neck, jaw, back or upper belly.
- Breathlessness, cold sweat, nausea, light-headedness.
- Women, older people and people with diabetes may only feel tired, breathless or vaguely unwell.

## While waiting
1. Sit them down, loosen clothing, reassure.
2. Give aspirin only if **115 or a health worker tells you to** and they aren't allergic to it.
3. Unresponsive and not breathing normally: start CPR.

## Sources
- American Heart Association — Warning signs of a heart attack

=== dengue
# Dengue — warning signs
> The dangerous phase is usually days 3–7, **as the fever comes down**. Watch closely.

!! Go to hospital at once with any warning sign below.

## Warning signs
- Severe belly pain, tenderness under the right ribs.
- Persistent vomiting, can't keep fluids down.
- Bleeding gums or nose, vomiting blood, black stools, prolonged periods.
- Drowsiness, restlessness, irritability.
- Cold hands and feet, passing little urine.

## Home care (no warning signs)
- Bring fever down **with paracetamol only**; **no** aspirin or ibuprofen.
- Drink plenty: oral rehydration salts, fruit juice, rice soup.
- Keep follow-up appointments and blood tests.

## Sources
- Vietnam Ministry of Health — Dengue diagnosis and treatment guideline (Decision 2760/QĐ-BYT, 2023)
- WHO — Dengue guidelines

=== child_fever
# Fever in children — when to see a doctor
> Fever is the body's response; how the child looks matters more than the number.

!! Call 115 / go to emergency if the child has a seizure, is very drowsy, struggles to breathe or turns blue.

## See a doctor now if
- A baby **under 3 months** has 38°C or more.
- Very drowsy, inconsolable, not feeding, vomiting everything.
- A rash that **doesn't fade when pressed** (visible through a glass).
- Stiff neck, bulging soft spot.
- Signs of dehydration: dry mouth, no tears, no urine for 8 hours.
- Fever for more than **2–3 days**, or with ear pain or painful urination.

## Home care
- Light clothing, more fluids / breastfeeding.
- Paracetamol by **weight**, as the label or doctor says; note the time of each dose.
- **No** aspirin for children; don't sponge with alcohol or wrap up warmly.

## Sources
- WHO — Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI)
- NICE — Fever in under 5s (NG143)

=== measure_bp
# Measuring blood pressure correctly
> Rest 5 minutes, sit correctly, take 2 readings 1–2 minutes apart.

## Before
- No coffee, smoking or exercise in the **30 minutes** before.
- Empty your bladder. Sit quietly for **5 minutes**.

## Position
1. Sit with your back supported, **feet flat**, legs uncrossed.
2. Arm resting on a table, **cuff at heart level**, on bare skin, 2–3 cm above the elbow.
3. Use the right cuff size (too small reads falsely high).
4. **Don't talk** or move while measuring.

## Measure and record
- Take **2 readings** 1–2 minutes apart; record both (or the average).
- Measure in the **morning** (before medicines and breakfast) and **evening**, same times daily.
- First time, measure both arms; then use the arm with the higher reading.

## Sources
- ESH 2023 — Guidelines for the management of arterial hypertension
- WHO — HEARTS technical package

=== measure_spo2
# Measuring SpO2 correctly
> Warm hands, sit still, wait at least 30 seconds for a steady number.

## How
1. Rest for a few minutes. Warm cold hands.
2. Remove nail polish or false nails on that finger.
3. Clip the oximeter to the **index or middle finger**, hand at heart level, keep still.
4. **Wait at least 30 seconds** until the number stops changing, then read it.

## Notes
- Cold hands, shaking, movement and bright light cause errors.
- Oximeters can read **higher than real** on darker skin — don't rely on the number alone if the person is breathless.
- People with chronic lung disease may have a personal target set by their doctor.

## Sources
- WHO — Pulse oximetry training manual
- FDA — Pulse oximeter accuracy and limitations

=== measure_temp
# Measuring temperature correctly
> Use the right place for the age, wait long enough, read the thermometer correctly.

## Where
- **Children under 5:** armpit (digital) or ear (infrared, over 4 weeks old).
- **Older children and adults:** mouth (under the tongue, lips closed), ear, forehead, armpit.
- Armpit readings are usually about **0.5°C lower** than mouth/ear.

## Notes
- Wait **15–30 minutes** after eating, drinking, bathing or exercise.
- Mercury thermometers: stop using them (toxic if broken) — use a digital one.
- Generally, **fever is 38°C or higher**.

## Sources
- NICE — Fever in under 5s (NG143)

=== bp_levels
# What counts as high blood pressure?
> Measure correctly, over several days. One high reading isn't a diagnosis.

## Easy markers (adults)
- **Below 130/85:** normal.
- **140/90 or higher:** high — re-measure over several days; see a doctor if it stays high.
- **180/120 or higher:** very high — rest 5 minutes and re-measure; if still high or with chest pain, severe headache, breathlessness, weakness or blurred vision — **call 115**.

## In the app
The app flags "attention" from **140/90** and "urgent" from **180/120**.

## Sources
- ESH 2023 — Guidelines for the management of arterial hypertension

=== spo2_levels
# What does low SpO2 mean?
> Normal SpO2 is 95–100%. 94% or lower needs attention; 90% or lower is an emergency.

## Markers
- **95–100%:** normal.
- **91–94%:** low — re-measure correctly; if still low or breathless, contact a doctor.
- **90% or lower:** dangerous — call 115, especially with breathlessness, blue lips or drowsiness.

## Notes
- Look at the **person**, not just the number: fast or hard breathing, blue lips and drowsiness are serious even with a "good" reading.
- People with chronic lung disease (COPD) may have a target of 88–92% set by their doctor.

## In the app
The app flags "attention" at **≤ 94%** and "urgent" at **≤ 90%**.

## Sources
- WHO — Pulse oximetry training manual

=== paracetamol
# Using paracetamol safely
> Paracetamol is safe at the right dose, but overdose causes serious liver damage. Add up every medicine containing it.

## Rules
- Follow the label or prescription; doses **at least 4–6 hours** apart.
- Healthy adults usually **no more than 4 g a day** (8 × 500 mg tablets); lower for people who drink alcohol, have liver disease or are frail — ask a doctor.
- **Children: dose by weight**, use children's syrup/sachets and the measuring syringe or cup provided.
- Many cold and cough medicines already contain paracetamol — **read the ingredients** to avoid doubling up.

## Emergency
- Suspected overdose (even without symptoms) — call 115 / go to hospital now, bring the packaging.

## Sources
- Vietnam National Drug Formulary — Paracetamol
- WHO Model Formulary

=== antibiotics
# Antibiotics — use them right
> Antibiotics don't cure colds or flu caused by viruses. Only take them when a doctor prescribes.

## Four rules
- **Don't** buy them yourself, use leftovers, or take someone else's prescription.
- Take the **full course** as prescribed, even if you feel better.
- If you've had an antibiotic allergy, add it under **Allergies** in the record so the app can warn you.
- Hives, swollen lips or breathlessness after a dose — stop, **call 115 if breathless**.

## Sources
- WHO — Antimicrobial resistance fact sheet

=== expired_medicine
# Disposing of expired medicines safely
> Don't flush medicines or throw whole blister packs in the bin. Return them to a pharmacy or clinic if you can.

## How
1. Ask your pharmacy or health station whether they **take back medicines**.
2. At home: take medicines out of the packs, **mix with coffee grounds or soil**, seal in a bag, then bin it.
3. Scratch out **names** on boxes and prescriptions.
4. Aerosols and inhalers: don't burn or puncture them.

## Check the cabinet regularly
- Discoloured, runny or odd-smelling medicines: throw away even if in date.
- Opened syrups and eye drops: use within the time on the label (often 28–90 days) — the app tracks this if you record the "opened on" date.

## Sources
- WHO — Guidelines for safe disposal of unwanted pharmaceuticals
- U.S. FDA — Disposal of unused medicines

=== heat_stroke
# Heat exhaustion and heatstroke in older people and children
> Older people, young children and people with chronic illness overheat easily. Cool them down at once; call 115 if confused.

!! Call 115 if they're confused, drowsy, having a seizure, or hot and red with no sweating.

## Signs
- Dizziness, headache, nausea, exhaustion, cramps.
- Severe: very high body temperature, hot red skin, confusion, fainting.

## What to do
1. Move to a **cool, airy** place; remove extra clothing.
2. **Cool down**: sponge or spray with cool water and fan; cool cloths on the neck, armpits and groin.
3. If alert: small sips of water or oral rehydration salts. If drowsy: **don't** give drinks.

## Prevention
- Drink regularly **even when not thirsty**; avoid the sun 11:00–15:00.
- Check daily on older relatives living alone during heatwaves.

## Sources
- WHO — Heat and health

=== minor_cuts
# Scrapes and small cuts
> Rinse with clean water, stop the bleeding, cover it. Most heal in a few days.

## Do now
1. Wash your hands, then **rinse the wound under clean running water** for a few minutes; soap around it.
2. Press gently with clean gauze to stop bleeding.
3. Mild antiseptic (diluted povidone-iodine) or saline.
4. Cover with a plaster or gauze; change when wet or dirty.

## See a doctor if
- The cut is deep, gaping, longer than 1–2 cm, on the face, or still bleeding after 10 minutes of pressure.
- The wound is dirty (rusty nail, soil) and no tetanus shot in 10 years — ask about a booster.
- Redness, warmth, pus or fever after a few days (infection).

## Sources
- IFRC — International First Aid Guidelines 2020

=== sprain
# Sprains and twisted ankles
> Rest – ice – compress – elevate for the first 1–2 days.

## Do now
1. **Rest** the injured part.
2. **Ice** for 15–20 minutes every 2–3 hours (ice wrapped in a cloth, never directly on skin).
3. **Compress** with an elastic bandage — not too tight (numbness or blue skin means too tight).
4. **Elevate** above the heart when lying down.
5. No hard massage or hot compresses in the first 2 days.

## See a doctor if
- Deformity, can't bear weight, severe pain, rapid swelling.
- Numbness, coldness or blue skin below the injury.
- Not better after 3–5 days.

## Sources
- IFRC — International First Aid Guidelines 2020

=== insect_sting
# Bee stings and insect bites
> Remove the sting, wash, apply cold. The real danger is **anaphylaxis** — breathlessness, swollen lips or face.

!! Call 115 now for breathlessness, swollen lips/tongue/throat, hives all over, fainting, or many stings.

## Do now
1. Move away from the bees. Honeybee: **scrape the sting out** with a card edge (don't squeeze the venom sac).
2. Wash with soap and clean water.
3. **Apply cold** for 10–15 minutes.
4. Watch for signs of a severe allergy for at least **30–60 minutes**.

## See a doctor if
- Stung in the mouth, throat or eye.
- A child or older person with many stings (wasps, hornets).
- Swelling spreads or pain worsens after 1–2 days.

## Sources
- IFRC — International First Aid Guidelines 2020
- WHO — Anaphylaxis

=== animal_bite
# Dog or cat bites and scratches
> Wash with soap and running water for **15 minutes**, then get rabies vaccination the same day.

!! Rabies is almost always fatal once symptoms appear but is **preventable** with timely vaccination. Don't wait to observe the animal first.

## Do now
1. **Wash with soap and running water for at least 15 minutes.**
2. Disinfect with 70% alcohol or povidone-iodine.
3. Don't stitch it closed, apply leaves, or squeeze blood out.
4. **Get rabies vaccination** (and ask about a tetanus booster) at the nearest clinic.
5. Note the animal (pet or stray, vaccinated or not) and observe it for 10 days — as the doctor advises.

## Sources
- WHO — Rabies fact sheet

=== diarrhea
# Diarrhoea and dehydration
> Replacing fluids with **correctly mixed** oral rehydration salts matters most. Keep children eating/breastfeeding.

## Do now
- Drink **ORS mixed exactly as the sachet says** (too strong or too weak both harm), sip after each loose stool.
- Children: breastfeed more, keep eating; doctors often add **zinc**.
- Don't give anti-diarrhoea medicine to children; no antibiotics unless prescribed.

## See a doctor now if
- Signs of **dehydration**: dry lips, very thirsty or too drowsy to drink, sunken eyes, little or no urine.
- Blood in the stool, high fever, vomiting everything.
- Babies under 6 months, older people, or diarrhoea lasting over 2 days.

## Sources
- WHO — Diarrhoeal disease fact sheet
- WHO — The treatment of diarrhoea (ORS, zinc)

=== eye_injury
# Dust or chemicals in the eye
> Rinse the eye with clean running water. Chemicals: rinse continuously for at least 15–20 minutes, then see a doctor.

!! Chemicals (lime, bleach, caustic soda) in the eye: rinse immediately and continuously, then go to eye emergency.

## Dust or small particles
1. Don't rub.
2. Blink a lot, or rinse with clean water / saline.
3. Something stuck in the eye: **don't remove it**, cover both eyes loosely, see a doctor now.

## Chemicals
1. Rinse at once with clean water **running continuously for 15–20 minutes**, holding the eyelids open, head tilted so water runs away from the other eye.
2. Remove contact lenses.
3. Bring the chemical container to the doctor.

## Sources
- IFRC — International First Aid Guidelines 2020
